Train the fly

The simple reflex is a rule I wrote by hand: turn toward whichever way the motion detectors say the world is moving. A trained decoder can do better. The fly watches four other worlds spin at random speeds while its brain stays exactly as published. Least-squares regression then learns 8 weights, one per motion-detector type, that turn their activity into an estimate of how fast the fly is spinning. The fly turns against that estimate.

Training and the test flights take about 15 seconds on a laptop. The flight pauses meanwhile.

      What's real here?

      Real

      The fly's visual system is the flyvis model from Lappalainen et al. (Nature, 2024): 45,669 neurons wired from its connectome, the same network as on the email page. Every response you see comes from simulating it.

      Added for the simulation

      The world, the wind and the steering rules. Only turning is simulated: no forward flight and no inertia. In a real fly, lobula plate cells pool the T4/T5 signals and descending neurons drive the wings. Those aren't in the model, so the simple reflex and the trained decoder stand in for them.

      What to expect

      Steering usually cuts the spin by 30–50%. The fly can't cancel it completely: its neurons react with a delay, and in strong wind the world moves faster than its motion detectors can track.
